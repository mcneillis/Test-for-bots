### A1. Who is controller, and who is processor

**External studies (the design these notes assume; Sara decides).** Prolific is controller of account data. The researcher is a separate controller of participant data it receives and of study responses. Prolific says it does not see or store those responses.

Researcher Terms, section N, Data Protection:

> “Personal data related to you, like your account registration information, contact details and any correspondence with us, will be held by us and used in accordance with our Privacy Notice. We are a data controller in relation to that personal data.”

> “You will be a data controller in your own right in relation to that personal data, and it is your responsibility to make sure that your collection and use of that personal data complies with applicable law.”

> “When you conduct Studies off-platform, you will receive Study responses from the Participant.”

Help centre, “Prolific supplier/vendor information”, 23 June 2026, https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445120-prolific-supplier-vendor-information:

> “No, we do not act as a data processor. All of the personal data associated with any user’s account (whether participants or researchers) is personal data of which Prolific is a controller, as we process it for our own business purposes (primarily the purposes of offering our platform services to users concerned). We do not host studies or otherwise process data on behalf of our researcher users.”

Help centre, “IRB applications and ethics approval”, 4 August 2026, https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/449628-irb-applications-and-ethics-approval:

> “Prolific can only see the name and description for your study, and any other inputted data within the Prolific app, such as demographic filters selected and the link to your study. Prolific cannot see or store the responses participants give you.”

**Survey Builder is the exception.** If questions are hosted inside Prolific, Prolific says it becomes the researcher’s processor for submissions, and the researcher remains controller.

Survey Builder supplementary terms:

> “We’re a data processor. In relation to Participants’ Submissions, we are acting as your data processor for the purposes of applicable data protection law.”

**For Sara to decide.** One design that matches these terms is to run the insomnia questions, the pregnancy screen, and the app task on software outside Prolific, and to use Prolific to recruit, message, and pay. The ID is a pseudonym. Prolific is explicit that it is still personal data.

Researcher Terms, section N:

> “You shall not in the context of any Study or otherwise request any direct identifiers from Participants.”

> “The participant information provided by Prolific is pseudonymized. This means participants are identified only by their Prolific ID rather than by direct identifiers such as their name, email address, or postal address.”

> “Each participant is assigned a unique Participant ID (24-character alphanumeric unique identifier). This means their data is pseudonymized and only Prolific will know their identity.”

> “We will not identify participants to you, but will refer only to Prolific IDs which are a string of numbers and letters. You must not attempt to re-identify participants or ask them for any direct identifiers.”

Researchers must record the ID. Prolific does not join it to the external dataset for them.

IDs article:

> “You must record the Prolific ID of every participant who takes part in your study.”

> “There are two common ways to do this: Automatically through URL parameters - this is the recommended approach. By asking participants for their Prolific ID directly in your survey.”

**Opinion, for Sara to accept or reject.** One way to match the IDs article is to pass the Prolific ID into the external study by URL parameter, store it with the Lazoo record, and not add name, email, phone, date of birth, or address. ### A6. Retention and deletion

Three different clocks apply. They are easy to mix up.

**1. Off-platform study data.** Prolific says it does not keep it. The sponsor sets the period, tells participants, and deletes.

Privacy article, 11 December 2025:

> “We do not store any data provided within studies. These are carried out on external survey software/platforms.”

> “We won’t keep your data for longer than necessary.”

Participants’ personal data article, 5 August 2026, requires the researcher’s notice to say “How long you'll keep it” and “How participants can exercise any applicable privacy rights.”

Getting consent, 18 June 2026, requires the consent form to explain “How the data will be stored and how long it will be retained” and “How participants can withdraw their consent and request the deletion of their data.”

Researcher Terms, section N, on the SCC annex, tie the transfer duration to “your organisation or institution’s retention policy” and to “no longer than is necessary”.

**2. Prolific account data.** Inactive participant accounts are closed and deleted at 14 months.

Participant help centre, 26 June 2026:

> “For participants, this means that if your account has been inactive for 14 months, your account will be permanently closed and your data deleted. You'll receive email notifications ahead of this deadline — first at 6 months, then at 12 months of inactivity.”

“Data Deletion Notice FAQ”, https://participant-help.prolific.com/en/articles/449632-data-deletion-notice-faq, says the same 14-month end point, and that account history and “About you” answers are then erased. The researcher privacy article, 11 December 2025, uses looser wording: “personal data is deleted within a reason period after account closure”. The 14-month rule is the specific one.

**3. Survey Builder submissions.** The researcher deletes them, or Prolific deletes them when the researcher account ends.

Survey Builder terms:

> “The duration of our processing is however long you choose to keep those Submissions hosted in our platform. You can delete them at any time using functions of Survey Builder.”

> “If your account with Prolific is terminated for any reason, we will delete Submissions hosted in our platform.”

**For Sara to decide.** Prolific’s 14-month deletion does not delete App Ltd’s copy of the special category data. The protocol and the participant notice need App Ltd’s own period, which Sara sets. Prolific also says demographic exports may disappear if the participant closes the account or withdraws a screener, so a later re-download is not assured. Section N: “We do not guarantee that this basic demographic information and performance information will be continually or indefinitely available, as its availability may depend on Participant account and consent settings.”

### A7. Clinical studies, medical-device studies, and ethics

**No Prolific page was found that bans clinical investigations or medical-device studies by name.** That absence is **not confirmed** as permission. What is published is a short list of unsuitable studies, plus a general duty to obey the law and the sponsor’s ethics requirements.

**Under-18, clinic visits, and posted devices — primary Prolific page.** “Are there types of studies that aren't suitable for Prolific?”, page date 5 August 2026, https://researcher-help.prolific.com/en/articles/445105-are-there-types-of-studies-that-aren-t-suitable-for-prolific. **Date read: 12 October 2026.**

> “Prolific does not permit studies that recruit participants under the age of 18. This restriction applies even with parental permission or for studies where parents and children participate together.”

> “Studies involving in-person activities are also prohibited. This includes asking participants to visit specific locations or sending them items to use during the study.”

The same page, same date read, also says studies that require personally identifiable information “are unlikely to be supported”. A clinic visit is the “visit specific locations” sentence. Posting a device or other item to a participant is the “sending them items to use during the study” sentence. No other primary Prolific page was needed for these three rules.

Also unsuitable: non-monetary rewards, and unfair screening or rejection. Minimum pay on the IRB article, 4 August 2026, is £6 / $8 per hour, with a recommendation of at least £9 / $12 per hour. Payment must go through Prolific.

Researcher Terms, quick summary:

> “When you use Prolific, you have to treat participants with fairness and respect, in accordance with good research practice, applicable law and your institution’s ethics policies.”

Full terms, in the researcher-conduct section:

> “In using Prolific and conducting Studies, you must also comply with applicable law, and any ethics policies, research protocols or other similar requirements of your employing institution or organisation.”

Survey Builder terms:

> “your obligations to comply with applicable law and with your own institution’s ethics approvals are all the same, just as if you were posting an external study link.”

The sensitive-content article expects a debrief that tells the participant who else to contact, “likely [the] Institutional Review Board that gave ethical approval”.

Prolific’s own research-ethics article on prolific.com is general education. It says most human research needs an IRB or REC, and that the need “depends on factors including participant risk level, study setting, and institutional policies.” It is not a Prolific rule that a university REC is enough for a device investigation, and it is not a rule that ethics review can be skipped. URL: https://www.prolific.com/resources/ethical-considerations-in-research-best-practices-and-examples, accessed 12 October 2026.

**For Sara to decide.** The unsuitable-studies page does not, on the text read, name clinical investigations or medical-device studies as a banned category. It does bar under-18 recruitment, visits to a location, and sending items to use in the study. A favourable ethics opinion does not, on the identifiers page, authorise direct identifiers. Prolific’s pages do not supply a Research Ethics Service opinion or an MHRA notice. Whether the listing goes ahead stays with Sara.

### The statute that matters is regulation 16, not regulation 29

**Legal requirement.** Lazoo, as smartphone software for insomnia, is a general medical device if it meets the regulation 2 definition. It is not an active implantable device. Notification for a general medical device in Great Britain is **regulation 16**, in Part II. **Regulation 29**, in Part III, is the same style of duty for **active implantable** devices. Using regulation 29 as the duty for Lazoo would be the wrong Part.

MHRA guidance often writes “regulations 16 and 29” because one flowchart covers both families. That citation is guidance shorthand. It does not move Lazoo into Part III.

Regulation 16(1), Great Britain text, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/618/regulation/16, accessed 12 October 2026:

> “Subject to paragraph (2), no person shall supply a relevant device (if that supply is also a making available of the device) for the purposes of a clinical investigation in Great Britain unless, before he does so, the manufacturer of the device or their UK responsible person has given at least 60 days prior notice in writing to the Secretary of State of the intended investigation…”

The notice must be the statement required by sections 1 and 2.2 of Annex VIII, read with Regulation (EU) No 722/2012, plus an undertaking to keep the section 3.2 Annex VIII documentation for at least five years.

Regulation 16(10):

> “No person shall conduct a clinical investigation of a relevant device otherwise than in accordance with Annex X; and otherwise than in accordance with any conditions imposed by the Secretary of State…”

The manufacturer must keep available the report referred to in section 2.3.7 of Annex X. The Annex X text itself was not re-read for this note. The section number is the one the regulation prints.

Regulation 16(2) is the marked-device exception:

> “Paragraph (1) shall not apply in respect of an intended clinical investigation of a relevant device that bears a UK marking otherwise than in breach of regulation 13, unless the aim of the intended investigation is to determine whether the device may be used for a purpose other than that in respect of which it was UK marked in accordance with regulation 13.”

Regulation 29(1) uses the same “supply” and “60 days prior notice” structure, for an active implantable, and points at Annex 6 and Annex 7 rather than Annex VIII and Annex X. https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/618/regulation/29, accessed 12 October 2026. It is quoted here so the Part is clear. It is not the Lazoo duty.

**What “clinical investigation” means in the statute.** Regulation 2 does **not**, on the text read, contain a free-standing definition of the words “clinical investigation”. It does define **“intended for clinical investigation”**, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/618/regulation/2, accessed 12 October 2026:

> “(a) intended for use by a registered medical practitioner when conducting investigations of that device in an adequate human clinical environment; or (b) intended for use by any other person in Great Britain who, by virtue of their professional qualification, is authorised to carry out investigations of that device in an adequate human clinical environment.”

That definition is the placing-on-the-market category for investigational devices. Whether it also limits the regulation 16 duty is **not confirmed** by a case or by MHRA. It is the main statutory hook for the “no clinician, no clinic” argument below.

**Medical device and intended purpose — regulation 2, date read 12 October 2026.** https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2002/618/regulation/2. Great Britain revised text:

> “medical device” means any instrument, apparatus, appliance, software, material or other article, whether used alone or in combination, together with any accessories, including the software intended by its manufacturer to be used specifically for diagnosis or therapeutic purposes or both and necessary for its proper application, which— (a) is intended by the manufacturer to be used for human beings for the purpose of— (i) diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, treatment or alleviation of disease, (ii) diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of or compensation for an injury or handicap, (iii) investigation, replacement or modification of the anatomy or of a physiological process, or (iv) control of conception; and (b) does not achieve its principal intended action in or on the human body by pharmacological, immunological or metabolic means, even if it is assisted in its function by such means…
