## Plain English summary

Sleep services still ask people to fill in a paper diary. The diary is easy to abandon, and the record is hard to check. This project develops two monitoring tools. It does not diagnose and it does not treat.

The first is a phone diary for adults with insomnia. The person records the night. The diary works out the usual sleep times by ordinary arithmetic and can share that record with a clinician, who decides what it means. It does not tell the person what is wrong, and it does not offer therapy. The version in hand does only that recording and arithmetic. Marking for the United Kingdom and for Europe, with a representative in the European Union, is planned. Neither marking is in place. ### Background and rationale

Adult insomnia is usually recorded first on a paper morning diary. A paper diary is easy to leave unfinished and hard to audit. This award develops Lazoo, a monitoring diary, and starts the skin logger as a separate product. It does not diagnose and it does not treat. Dr Sara McNeillis decides the final names.

Under the Medical Devices Regulations 2002 the diary is put forward as a medical device. The protocol’s working position is Class IIa. That class is not a final view of the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency. For the evidence standards of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, a tool that records data, calculates a result, and shares it with a clinician for later review fits Tier C, “inform clinical management”, without any diagnosis claim. A diary whose data stayed only with the user would sit in Tier B, which this programme does not fund. This project is written so that the record goes to the clinician. In Table 2 of that framework, “inform clinical management” maps to Class I unless the condition is critical, so the working Class IIa needs a stated basis in monitoring terms. That basis is to be confirmed by Dr Sara McNeillis and a regulatory adviser. The logger’s class is open in the design pack and is not stated here.

The build in hand stores the diary and calculates standard sleep measures by fixed arithmetic. Artificial intelligence and decision support are out of scope for this award. Marking for the United Kingdom and for Europe is planned, with a representative in the European Union, around the draft 2026 amendment to the medical device rules. What that draft does to class is to be confirmed against the final instrument. Neither marking is in place, and the representative is not appointed. The clinical safety standard for health information technology, the Digital Technology Assessment Criteria, and the Data Security and Protection Toolkit are not in place. The planned approach is to map the assessment criteria and the guide to good practice for digital and data-driven health technologies during the award, record the gaps, and close the gaps this award can close. That mapping has not been done. This outline does not claim that either product meets those standards.

The diary study is LAZ-VAL-001. Protocol version 0.5 and the unsigned version 0.6.1 agree on the design, and the team must confirm which text it relies on. Heart rate stays out of the study. Adults aged 18 or over complete Lazoo and a Consensus Sleep Diary–Morning together, for fourteen mornings. The primary quantity is the night-level difference in calculated total sleep time, in minutes, by Bland–Altman mean bias and 95 per cent limits of agreement, with nights clustered in the person. There is no diagnostic cut-point. The feasibility floor is 30 completers, which the protocol says is not a powered sample. The analysis plan is not signed.

### Aims and objectives

The aim is to prepare the diary for monitoring adult insomnia, with the record shared for a clinician to review later, and, in the same award, to build the first logger prototypes at the company so that a later evaluation can be designed. The aim is not to diagnose insomnia, to detect sleep apnoea, or to deliver therapy. Artificial intelligence and decision support are out of scope for this award.

The objectives are to fix the diary, the calculations, the report and the screen. They are to estimate agreement on calculated total sleep time, with heart rate, the logger, actigraphy and pregnancy outside the study. They are to build and bench-test the logger at the company, inside the freedom-to-operate rules. No one wears the logger in this study, and no wearing protocol exists. They are to map the digital assessment criteria and the good-practice guide, and to assemble the records a later marking file will need. Marking is not in place. They are to pay a group of people with sleep problems to shape the words and the screens. That has not started. Adoption, intellectual property and a health-economic case are deliverables, and the numbers are to be confirmed.

Success for the diary is the Bland–Altman description of total sleep time, completeness, an intact audit trail, and usability. Any further threshold is to be confirmed. Success for the logger is to be confirmed when the design is frozen. The exit point is that agreement study, bench-tested prototypes, and a written route towards marking. The target technology readiness level is to be confirmed. End users are adults keeping a sleep diary and the clinicians who read the record. A study in which someone wears the logger needs its own protocol.
