## Plain English summary

Sleep services still ask people to fill in a paper diary. The diary is easy to abandon, and the record is hard to check. This project develops two monitoring tools. It does not diagnose and it does not treat.

The first is a phone diary for adults with insomnia. The person records the night. The diary works out the usual sleep times by ordinary arithmetic and can share that record with a clinician, who decides what it means. It does not tell the person what is wrong, and it does not offer therapy. The version in hand does only that recording and arithmetic. Marking for the United Kingdom and for Europe, with a representative in the European Union, is planned. Neither marking is in place.

The second is a reusable skin logger, with a fresh adhesive for each wear of fourteen days. It is meant to record temperature and movement for the wearer to see and, if they choose, to share. It does not diagnose, predict, treat, or decide care. A related company will build the prototypes. The NHS organisation will not build them. Several design choices are still open. The logger is not worn in the diary study.

Over the award the phone diary will be compared with a standard paper morning diary in adults. The study is about insomnia. Heart rate is not collected. Pregnancy is excluded. People are recruited through a research-volunteer service, not from NHS lists. In the same award the related company will design and bench-test the logger. A group of people with experience of sleep problems will be paid to shape the diary, the logger and the words used in the study. That involvement has not started.

SLEEP & HEALTH CLINIC LTD would hold the award. A related company makes the diary and would build the logger. University College London Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust is the intended NHS organisation for the project.

### Background and rationale

Adult insomnia is usually recorded first on a paper morning diary. A paper diary is easy to leave unfinished and hard to audit. This award develops Lazoo, a monitoring diary, and starts the skin logger as a separate product. It does not diagnose and it does not treat.

Under the Medical Devices Regulations 2002 the diary is put forward as a medical device. The protocol’s working position is Class IIa. That class is not a final view of the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency. For the evidence standards of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, a tool that records data, calculates a result, and shares it with a clinician for later review fits Tier C, “inform clinical management”, without any diagnosis claim. A diary whose data stayed only with the user would sit in Tier B, which this programme does not fund. This project is written so that the record goes to the clinician. In Table 2 of that framework, “inform clinical management” maps to Class I unless the condition is critical, so the working Class IIa needs a stated basis in monitoring terms. That basis is to be confirmed by Dr Sara McNeillis and a regulatory adviser. The logger’s class is open in the design pack and is not stated here.

The build in hand stores the diary and calculates standard sleep measures by fixed arithmetic. Artificial intelligence and decision support are out of scope for this award. Marking for the United Kingdom and for Europe is planned, with a representative in the European Union, around the draft 2026 amendment to the medical device rules. What that draft does to class is to be confirmed against the final instrument. Neither marking is in place, and the representative is not appointed. The clinical safety standard for health information technology, the Digital Technology Assessment Criteria, and the Data Security and Protection Toolkit are not in place. The planned approach is to map the assessment criteria and the guide to good practice for digital and data-driven health technologies during the award, record the gaps, and close the gaps this award can close. That mapping has not been done. This outline does not claim that either product meets those standards.
