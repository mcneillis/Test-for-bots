## 2. UK law: which rule applies to standalone software

Regulation 7(1) of the Medical Devices Regulations 2002, in the version for England, Wales and Scotland, says devices "are classified as belonging to Class I, IIa, IIb or III in accordance with the classification criteria set out in Annex IX of Directive 93/42, read with Directive 2003/12 and Directive 2005/50".

Annex IX of Directive 93/42 has no rule written for software. Section 1.4 of that Annex says: "Stand alone software is considered to be an active medical device." Section 1.6 defines an active device for diagnosis as one used "to supply information for detecting, diagnosing, monitoring or treating physiological conditions, states of health, illnesses or congenital deformities". So standalone software such as Lazoo is classified under the rules for active devices.

The relevant rules are:

- **Rule 10.** "Active devices intended for diagnosis are in Class IIa: … if they are intended to allow direct diagnosis or monitoring of vital physiological processes, unless they are specifically intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters, where the nature of variations is such that it could result in immediate danger to the patient, for instance variations in cardiac performance, respiration, activity of CNS in which case they are in Class IIb."
- **Rule 11 (UK).** This rule is not about software. It reads: "All active devices intended to administer and/or remove medicines, body liquids or other substances to or from the body are in Class IIa, unless this is done in a manner … that is potentially hazardous … in which case they are in Class IIb." It does not apply to Lazoo.
- **Rule 12.** "All other active devices are in Class I."

The implementing rules also matter. Rule 2.1: "Application of the classification rules shall be governed by the intended purpose of the devices." Rule 2.2: "If the device is intended to be used in combination with another device, the classification rules shall apply separately to each of the devices. Accessories are classified in their own right separately from the device with which they are used." Rule 2.3: "Software, which drives a device or influences the use of a device, falls automatically in the same class." Rule 2.5: where several rules apply, "the strictest rules resulting in the higher classification shall apply".

**Verified result.** In current UK law for Great Britain, standalone software is an active device classified under Annex IX of Directive 93/42. The rule that applies to monitoring software is Rule 10, not a software rule 11. EU MDR Rule 11 does not apply in Great Britain.

**Possible change in UK law.** The MHRA published the draft Medical Devices (Amendment) Regulations 2026 for comment on 8 May 2026. Published commentary reports that adoption is expected around December 2026 and entry into force around June 2027, with transitional periods, and that the draft does not include a separate software rule like EU Rule 11, so software would still be classified under the active-device rules such as Rule 10. These points come from commentary, not from the draft text, and are to be confirmed against the final instrument.

Dated note, 24 October 2026: published commentary on the draft is mixed. ## 3. EU law: Rule 11 and MDCG 2019-11 rev.1

As quoted in MDCG 2019-11 rev.1, Rule 11 reads:

> Software intended to provide information which is used to take decisions with diagnosis or therapeutic purposes is classified as class IIa, except if such decisions have an impact that may cause: death or an irreversible deterioration of a person's state of health, in which case it is in class III; or a serious deterioration of a person's state of health or a surgical intervention, in which case it is classified as class IIb.
> Software intended to monitor physiological processes is classified as class IIa, except if it is intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters, where the nature of variations of those parameters is such that it could result in immediate danger to the patient, in which case it is classified as class IIb.
> All other software is classified as class I.

The guidance divides the rule into sub-rule 11a (information used for decisions with diagnostic or therapeutic purposes), 11b (monitoring of physiological processes) and 11c (everything else). On 11b it says that sub-rule "was introduced to ensure that MDSW which has the same intended purpose as (hardware) devices which would fall under rule 10, third indent, are in the same risk class", and that "Vital physiological processes and parameters include, for example, respiration, heart rate, cerebral functions, blood gases, blood pressure and body temperature."

Annex III of the guidance gives an illustrative table for sub-rule 11a. Software that "informs clinical management" falls in Class IIa in every healthcare situation, including critical ones. Software that "drives clinical management" in a critical situation, or that is used to "treat or diagnose" in a serious situation, falls in Class IIb.

On combined products, the guidance quotes implementing rule 3.3 of Annex VIII: "Software, which drives or influences the use of a device, shall fall within the same class as the device", and "If software is independent of any other device, it shall be classified in its own right". It adds that software which "both achieves its own intended purpose and also drives or influences the use of a (hardware) device for a medical purpose is classified on its own, based on the intended purpose achieved. In such a case, however, the risk class shall not be lower than the risk class of the hardware medical device." Implementing rule 3.5 says the strictest rule and sub-rule applies.

## 4. Reasoned view: does Class IIa hold?

Heart rate is a vital physiological parameter. The UK Rule 10 text gives "variations in cardiac performance" as its example of the Class IIb case, and MDCG 2019-11 rev.1 lists heart rate among vital physiological parameters. So adding heart rate moves Lazoo into territory where the line between Class IIa and Class IIb has to be drawn carefully.

On the wording proposed in LAZ-IP-001-AMD-01, the working view is that **Class IIa can hold**, for these reasons:

1. Under UK Rule 10, monitoring of vital physiological processes is Class IIa. Class IIb is reserved for devices "specifically intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters, where the nature of variations is such that it could result in immediate danger". The proposed wording presents heart rate only as a trend across nights and weeks, as context for insomnia monitoring, with no alarm, no threshold and no claim to detect danger. A trend reviewed later by a clinician is not, on its face, intended for monitoring where variations could result in immediate danger.
2. Under EU Rule 11, the same reasoning applies to sub-rule 11b, and under sub-rule 11a the information at most "informs clinical management" of insomnia, which the illustrative table places in Class IIa.
3. The adult insomnia population, used at home with later clinician review, is not a critical care setting.

A side point should be noted for Compliance. Under the UK rules, a diary that only records what the person types might arguably fall under Rule 12 (Class I), because it does not itself monitor a physiological process. Adding physiological data from a wearable makes Rule 10 (Class IIa) clearly relevant. This does not change the Class IIa working position decided on 5 October 2026, but LAZ-CL-001 does not currently name any classification rule, and when it is revised it should state UK Rule 10 for Great Britain, with EU Rule 11 relevant only for Northern Ireland and the European Union. To be confirmed by Compliance.

This view holds only while the wording guards in section 6 are kept.

## 5. What could push Lazoo towards Class IIb

**Plainly: any alarm, alert, or detection of a dangerous change in heart rate would push Lazoo towards Class IIb, under UK Rule 10 (the "immediate danger" limb) and under EU Rule 11 (sub-rule 11b, and the "serious deterioration" limb of sub-rule 11a).**

The specific risks are:

1. **Monitoring vital parameters where variations could cause immediate danger.** UK Rule 10 third indent and its software counterpart, EU sub-rule 11b. Real-time or near-real-time heart rate display, or any use during the night to watch for change, would move towards this limb.
2. **Decisions that could cause serious deterioration.** EU sub-rule 11a: if a clinician or the person might act on Lazoo's heart rate information in a way that, if the information were wrong, could lead to a serious deterioration of health, the class rises to IIb. Presenting heart rate as something to act on, rather than as context, raises this risk.
3. **Any alarm, alert, notification or threshold.** Any automatic flag of "high", "low" or "abnormal" heart rate, colour coding against limits, or prompts to seek help.
4. **Arrhythmia or atrial fibrillation detection.** Any rhythm analysis, irregular-rhythm notice or atrial fibrillation output is a diagnostic function about the heart, outside insomnia monitoring, and would need a separate classification; it is likely to be Class IIb or higher depending on the decision it informs (to be confirmed).
5. **Use in pregnancy.** Lazoo may be used in pregnancy. Pregnancy changes the healthcare situation, and heart rate changes in pregnancy may carry clinical significance for mother and baby. If heart rate were presented or used to inform decisions in pregnancy, the "serious situation" column of the MDCG Annex III table, and the serious-deterioration limb of 11a, become more relevant. Whether this changes the class is to be confirmed by regulatory review; the safer course is the wording guards plus a decision by Sara on whether heart rate is shown in pregnancy.
6. **Any measuring function.** If heart rate is presented as a measured value with stated accuracy for clinical use, Lazoo, or Lazoo with CBT-14, takes on a measuring function. Under the UK regime a device with a measuring function has additional conformity assessment requirements (to be confirmed against the 2002 Regulations; not read for this review). Presenting a trend, not a reading, avoids a measuring claim.
7. **The interplay with CBT-14 hardware.** CBT-14 is presented as a wellness product with a skin temperature trend claim. If heart rate from CBT-14 becomes part of Lazoo's medical purpose, CBT-14 may become an accessory to Lazoo, or the two may become a system. UK implementing rule 2.2 says accessories are classified in their own right. UK rule 2.3 and EU implementing rule 3.3 say software that drives or influences a device falls in the same class as that device, and MDCG 2019-11 rev.1 says software that has its own purpose and also drives or influences hardware cannot be classed lower than the hardware. So:
   - if Lazoo controls or configures CBT-14's sensing, Lazoo cannot be classed below CBT-14, and CBT-14 would need its own medical device classification;
   - if CBT-14's heart rate is intended by the manufacturer to enable Lazoo's medical purpose, CBT-14 may meet the definition of an accessory and lose its wellness status;
   - who places CBT-14 on the market, and under which name, decides who the manufacturer of each part is. To be confirmed.
   The Compliance gate of 24 October 2026 also records that the sensor part SFH 7050 is discontinued, which affects CBT-14 hardware planning but not this classification reasoning.

### 9.3 Is 24-hour heart rate monitoring of a vital physiological parameter?

Yes. MDCG 2019-11 rev.1 says: "Vital physiological processes and parameters include, for example, respiration, heart rate, cerebral functions, blood gases, blood pressure and body temperature." Recording heart rate across the whole day rather than overnight does not change what is being monitored; it increases how much of it is monitored. Lazoo presenting heart rate trends from CBT-14 is therefore monitoring of a vital physiological process.

In Great Britain this falls under Rule 10 of Annex IX of Directive 93/42 (section 2), third indent, which gives Class IIa for devices "intended to allow direct diagnosis or monitoring of vital physiological processes". In Northern Ireland and the European Union it falls under Rule 11 of Annex VIII of the EU Regulation, whose second paragraph reads, in the archived text: "Software intended to monitor physiological processes is classified as class IIa, except if it is intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters, where the nature of variations of those parameters is such that it could result in immediate danger to the patient, in which case it is classified as class IIb." The EU Rule 10 also covers active devices "intended to allow direct diagnosis or monitoring of vital physiological processes", with the same exception, and implementing rule 3.5 says that where several rules apply "the strictest rule and sub-rule resulting in the higher classification shall apply". On the current wording both EU rules give Class IIa, so the strictest-rule test does not raise the class.

### 9.4 Does the immediate-danger limb apply?

On the wording now proposed, the working view is that it does not. The Class IIb limb in both regimes is for devices "specifically intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters" (UK Rule 10) or "intended for monitoring of vital physiological parameters" (EU Rule 11) where "the nature of variations … could result in immediate danger to the patient". The argument that Lazoo falls outside it rests on how heart rate is presented, not on the parameter itself:

- **Trends only.** Heart rate is shown as a pattern over days and weeks, not as a current value to be watched.
- **Looking back, not watching.** The clinician sees heart rate only in a report the patient chooses to share, reviewed afterwards. No clinician watches heart rate in real time, and Lazoo is not designed so that anyone can respond to a change as it happens.
- **No prompts to act.** There are no alarms, alerts, notifications or thresholds, no rhythm output and no atrial fibrillation output.
- **Setting.** Adults with insomnia at home, not anaesthesia, intensive care or emergency care.

MDCG 2019-11 rev.1 draws the same line in its examples. "Medical devices including MDSW intended to be used for continuous surveillance of vital physiological processes in anaesthesia, intensive care or emergency care should be classified as class IIb", whereas devices "intended to be used to obtain readings of vital physiological signals in routine check-ups including monitoring at home should be classified as class IIa". Electronic thermometers and stethoscopes "may be classified as class IIa per Rule 10, third indent since body temperature and heart rate are considered decisive information for diagnosis (implementing rule 3.7), where the nature of the variations of these parameters would not result in immediate danger to the patient." (MDSW in the guidance means medical device software.)

Recording over 24 hours does raise one point to watch. A day-and-night record is closer to continuous monitoring than a nightly summary, so the absence of any live display and of any alerting has become the main support for Class IIa rather than a detail of design.

### 9.8 Is CBT-14 an accessory, or part of a system?

Article 2(2) of the EU Regulation defines an accessory as "an article which, whilst not being itself a medical device, is intended by its manufacturer to be used together with one or several particular medical device(s) to specifically enable the medical device(s) to be used in accordance with its/their intended purpose(s) or to specifically and directly assist the medical functionality of the medical device(s) in terms of its/their intended purpose(s)". Once heart rate is a medical device function of Lazoo and CBT-14 is the source of that heart rate, CBT-14 appears on its face to meet this definition, because it specifically enables Lazoo's heart rate function. Implementing rule 2.2 of Annex IX of Directive 93/42 in Great Britain, and implementing rule 3.2 of Annex VIII of the EU Regulation, under which accessories are classified in their own right, mean that CBT-14 would need its own classification, separate from Lazoo.

This has three consequences for the classification. First, CBT-14 can no longer be presented as a wellness product while it supplies heart rate for Lazoo's medical purpose, even though its own claim stays at skin temperature trends. ### 9.9 Working conclusion

**Class IIa holds as the working classification.** In Great Britain it rests on Rule 10, third indent, of Annex IX of Directive 93/42. In Northern Ireland and the European Union it rests on Rule 11, sub-rules 11b and 11a, and Rule 10 of Annex VIII of the EU Regulation, which all give Class IIa on the proposed wording. Recording over 24 hours and adding a core temperature estimate do not, on their own, cross into Class IIb, because the parameters are presented as trends looked at afterwards, at home, with no one watching in real time and no prompt to act. This matches the "monitoring at home" example in MDCG 2019-11 rev.1.

The conclusion depends on these guards being kept:

1. Heart rate, and any core temperature output, shown only as a trend.
2. No live or real-time display, and no alarms, alerts, notifications, colour bands or thresholds.
3. No heart rhythm output and no atrial fibrillation output.
4. Heart rate reaching the clinician only in a report the patient chooses to share and reviewed afterwards, with processing on the phone until then.
5. Heart rate hidden in pregnancy, by a control that works reliably.
6. No use in anaesthesia, intensive care, emergency care or other acute settings, and "not an emergency service" kept in the wording.
7. No diagnosis, including no diagnosis of circadian rhythm disorders.
8. CBT-14 itself not in a class higher than Class IIa, and Lazoo not driving it in a way that takes a higher class.

Lazoo would move to Class IIb if any of the following were added: real-time watching of heart rate or core temperature, or alerts about either; detection of abnormal heart rate, or abnormal patterns, that informs a physician (the MDCG example of a heartbeat app that detects abnormalities and informs a physician is Class IIb under sub-rule 11a); any heart rhythm or atrial fibrillation output; use in acute care; heart rate or core temperature used in pregnancy to inform decisions; or any use where wrong information could lead to decisions causing a serious deterioration of health.

The class to be stated in LAZ-CL-001, when it is revised, is to be confirmed by Compliance, and the core temperature question in 9.5 has to be answered first.
